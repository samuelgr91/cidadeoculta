# The Hill the Governor Ordered Burned

Anyone looking up towards the Serra de Ouro Preto today will find ruins scattered among paths, vegetation and old mining structures.

The place's name already tells part of its story: Morro da Queimada, the Burned Hill.

It did not acquire that name because of an accidental fire. In 1720, during one of Vila Rica's first major political crises, Governor D. Pedro de Almeida Portugal, the Count of Assumar, ordered the suppression of a movement known as the Vila Rica Sedition or Felipe dos Santos Revolt.

The government's response directly affected the mining settlement on the hillside. Houses were demolished. Properties were burned. The memory of the fire became part of the place's name.

## Before the fire

Morro da Queimada was important even before the revolt. Settlement began in the first decade of the eighteenth century, as mining expanded towards higher areas of the Serra de Ouro Preto.

As gold extraction moved beyond riverbeds, mining grounds, paths, lanes, homes, shops and places of worship appeared. What we now see as an archaeological site was a living settlement closely connected to mining.

The area had several names, including Morro do Ouro Podre and Morro do Pascoal Silva. The latter referred to Pascoal da Silva Guimarães, a wealthy, influential man connected to mining and one of the central figures in the crisis of 1720.

## What was happening in Vila Rica

The revolt did not arise from a single cause. In the early 1720s, tensions ran high between influential groups in the mining region and the colonial government.

Points of conflict included the Crown's fiscal policy, the introduction of royal gold foundries, the expulsion of certain clergy from mining areas, disputes over local administration and the reorganisation of military forces.

The foundries were particularly sensitive. Gold would have to be melted and officially controlled before circulating, increasing the Crown's ability to monitor production and collect the quinto, its one-fifth tax. Parts of the population and the mining elites regarded these measures as threats to local interests.

## The night the revolt began

According to Ouro Preto's Municipal Environment Department, the movement's outbreak occurred on the night of 28 June 1720. More than two thousand rebels reportedly marched towards Vila do Carmo, now Mariana, where the governor was staying.

The rebels put pressure on the Count of Assumar and presented their demands. At that moment, he accepted some of them. But the crisis was not over. After reorganising his forces, Assumar began the repression.

## The governor's return

The municipal source records that on 16 July 1720, the Count of Assumar reached Vila Rica at the head of around 1,500 men. The repression targeted participants and the movement's principal leaders.

Pascoal da Silva Guimarães, associated with the hillside mining settlement and identified by historical interpretations as a leading figure in the sedition, saw his properties targeted.

Ouro Preto's municipal government describes the episode directly: the settlement was destroyed by a large fire. Another official municipal page records the demolition and burning of houses belonging to Pascoal da Silva Guimarães and participants in the Sedition of 1720.

After this episode, the place became known as Morro da Queimada.

## Felipe dos Santos

Although popularly named after Felipe dos Santos, the movement involved several leaders and different social groups. Felipe became its best-known figure and was executed following the repression.

Other leaders were imprisoned, sent to Rio de Janeiro and subsequently exiled or prosecuted. Contemporary historical writing also draws attention to Pascoal da Silva Guimarães, an influential landowner and miner whose settlement was directly associated with the fire.

Reducing the entire revolt to one leader therefore obscures the conflict's complexity.

## The fire did not erase the place

There is an important detail: the fire of 1720 did not mean that all human occupation on Morro da Queimada immediately vanished. The municipal government records continued occupation throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

The present-day site therefore brings together remains from different periods. Identified structures include ruined houses, old mining areas, water reservoirs, stone walls and enclosures, paths and structures associated with gold extraction, and the remains of an old windmill.

Morro da Queimada is more than the setting of a revolt. It preserves material evidence of early urban settlement and mining during the formation of Vila Rica.

## An archaeological site above the city

Today the area forms part of the Morro da Queimada Ecomuseum and is recognised as an archaeological site. The municipal government highlights its importance as evidence of early urban forms in Minas Gerais and the gold rush at the beginning of the eighteenth century.

In 2008, the municipality created the Morro da Queimada Municipal Archaeological Park to preserve the area and its remains.

This changes the experience of visiting. We are not simply looking at a viewpoint over Ouro Preto. We are walking through a place that was mined, inhabited, politically contested, burned and inhabited again.

## What can still be seen

The strongest aspect of this story is that it left physical traces. Nearly three centuries later, ruins remain high on the hillside. Some are the remains of homes; others relate to mining work. Stone structures, paths and reservoirs help us understand the scale of the old settlement.

The story does not end in the past. There is something that can still be seen today. The ruins are the material link between the fire of 1720 and the present-day city, although each structure needs its own archaeological identification before being attributed to that fire.

## A historical distinction

Settlement, hamlet or properties on Morro do Pascoal Silva/Ouro Podre are more precise terms than neighbourhood for this historical episode. Official sources record the demolition and burning of houses belonging to Pascoal da Silva Guimarães and participants in the sedition, and also describe the destruction of the settlement.

The settlement that gave rise to today's Morro da Queimada is therefore the subject of this account.

## What is documented

The area was inhabited and worked by miners in the early eighteenth century. It was known as Morro do Ouro Podre and Morro do Pascoal Silva. The Vila Rica Sedition took place in 1720 and was suppressed by the Count of Assumar. Houses and properties connected to Pascoal da Silva Guimarães and participants in the revolt were demolished and burned. The episode gave rise to the name Morro da Queimada. Felipe dos Santos was executed after the repression. Occupation continued in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Ruins of homes and mining structures remain. The hill is now an archaeological site under municipal protection.

## What requires care

Felipe dos Santos was not the movement's only leader. The revolt cannot be reduced to a reaction to higher taxes. It is incorrect to claim that the entire area was destroyed and permanently abandoned in 1720. Every visible ruin cannot be directly attributed to the fire without specific archaeological identification. Settlement or hamlet is more historically precise than neighbourhood.

## Timeline

Early eighteenth century — The Serra de Ouro Preto becomes an important area of mining and settlement.

1710s–1720 — Political and fiscal tensions grow between local groups and the colonial government.

28 June 1720 — The municipal environment source places the outbreak of the sedition on this night.

June–July 1720 — Rebels pressure the governor in Vila do Carmo.

16 July 1720 — According to the municipal source, Assumar returns to Vila Rica with a military force and begins the repression.

1720 — Houses and properties are demolished and burned. The area becomes known as Morro da Queimada.

Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries — Occupation continues.

2008 — Creation of the Municipal Archaeological Park, under Municipal Law 465 of 29 December 2008.

2012 — The municipal environment page records the creation of the Municipal Archaeological Natural Monument. This is a separate protection designation from the park's creation in 2008.
